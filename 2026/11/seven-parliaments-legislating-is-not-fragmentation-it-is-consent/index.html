Seven parliaments legislating is not fragmentation. It is consent

Brussels wants one rulebook for addictive design, replacing laws that seven nations are already passing. A floor of minimum harmonisation we could accept. A regulation that wipes national law hands power to an institution no European voter can remove.

7 November 2026

Seven of our nations notified draft laws in 2025 and 2026 limiting minors' access to digital services. It is consent, working. On 2 November 2026 the Dutch MP Barbara Kathmann put before the House of Representatives a proposal to ban endless scroll, autoplay and notifications that lure users into payments, and nine days later the Commission is expected to table its Digital Fairness Act, negotiated in parallel with its KIDS Act proposal. The Commission has already warned that these national drafts fragment the single market. So the fight is not whether addictive design is regulated. It is whose law counts when the dust settles.

The case for the Dutch bill is strong on its own terms. Kathmann's proposal would make manipulative design tricks count as unfair commercial practices, covering social media, games, dating apps and chatbots, with a warning first, a fine second and, for repeat offenders, a switched-off platform. Kathmann herself says she would rather tackle harmful algorithms at European level, but that this takes too long, and she works with parliamentarians in seven other countries who want the same. That is what democratic pressure looks like: elected law-makers, accountable to their own voters, responding at the speed their voters expect. The Commission itself found in July 2026 that Meta's autoplay and infinite scroll push users into compulsive use, and in February 2026 it said the same of TikTok.

Notice instead what the harmonisers are doing with this. The Commission answered with a regulation, COM(2026) 681, that fixes child-access rules for the whole Union. France knows how this goes: the Commission rejected its first proposal for naming services, and its Constitutional Council struck down the earlier law as disproportionate. Kathmann says good laws proposed in Brussels are often lobbied away, and that there are more tech lobbyists than MEPs. If she half believes that, her conclusion is odd. She wants a national stopgap until Brussels takes over, which means handing the final word on addictive design to an institution her voters cannot remove and that she herself says lobbyists can bend.

The business objection is fair: firms serving several markets would face diverging rules, higher compliance costs and uneven protection for children. The answer is minimum harmonisation, not erasure. A common European floor removes the worst patchwork, while a member state that wants stricter rules for its children keeps them, and its voters can sack whoever imposed them. The price we accept is some extra cost for Dutch firms serving several markets. The price the harmonisers accept, and do not name, is that these rules are set where no European voter can dismiss anyone for them.

Two things must now happen. The Dutch House of Representatives should pass Kathmann's proposal, and the 25 governments that signed the Jutland Declaration should tell the Commission the same in writing: the Digital Fairness Act must be a floor, a minimum-harmonisation instrument that leaves member states free to keep or exceed stricter national rules on addictive design, not a regulation that wipes national law the day it applies. The missing piece was never one rulebook. It was the guarantee that the voters who live under these rules can reach and remove the people who wrote them, and that power stays with the parliaments that answer to those voters.
