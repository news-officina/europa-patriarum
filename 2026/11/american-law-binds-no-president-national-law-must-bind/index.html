A Europe of nations.

Opinion

American law binds no president. National law must bind Europe's defence.

An 86-vote sanctions act sat unused after an October waiver, and 400 million dollars authorized by Congress was delayed. A Congress that can be read around is not a guarantee; only national statutes are.

7 November 2026

The markets' reaction to the Democratic takeover of Congress on 3 November 2026 has done Europe a favour Washington's voters never intended: they have shown that American law is not a guarantee a European government can hold. On 3 November the Democrats won both chambers of Congress, and by 10:00 CET the next morning European defence stocks had fallen, with BAE Systems down 5.2 percent, Hensoldt down 4.9, Thales down 4.5 and Rheinmetall down 3.2, on fears a Democratic Congress would slow American military spending. The markets priced congressional law as Europe's safety net. It never was one.

The record of the past year settles what a Congress can and cannot do for us. In August 2026 the Senate passed the sanctions act named for the late Republican senator Lindsey Graham with 86 votes. On 16 October the president waived it, days after the Treasury lifted restrictions on Russian diesel purchases. Earlier in 2026 the Pentagon delayed 400 million dollars of Ukraine Security Assistance Initiative, money Congress had authorized, for months. Eighty-six senators voted and the law sat unused. The same holds where no vote exists at all: after the Supreme Court struck down the emergency tariffs in February 2026, the administration moved them under Section 301, which allows the president to act without a vote by lawmakers. If a veto-proof margin in the Senate holds for a president, a change of hands on the Speaker's gavel holds for nothing.

The next test is already scheduled. The Pentagon's force posture review for Europe, expected by December 2026, is likely to shift conventional military responsibility in NATO towards European ownership, while Congress has set a statutory floor on American troop numbers in Europe that the president contests. Congress wrote a floor into law and the administration argues with it. Undersecretary of war Elbridge Colby calls the shift NATO 3.0. An adviser's arithmetic on the money applies to the rest: ECFR policy fellow Nevada Joan Lee advises Europeans to treat earmarked funds as intent, not commitment. A law the president does not spend against is not a commitment. It is a sentence in a statute he has learned to read around.

A Democratic Congress is a real constraint, its defenders say: it can attach Ukraine money to must-pass bills such as the NDAA and dare the president to veto it, as Daniel Hamilton of the Brookings Institution suggests. Perhaps. James M. Lindsay of the Council on Foreign Relations notes that most lawmakers hesitate to oppose a president of their own party. A gavel change does not convert intent into commitment. October's waiver already proved it.

The duty therefore falls where the vote can reach it: home. Before the review lands, every government should put before its own parliament a draft law fixing the conventional NATO responsibilities its forces will carry and the national money to pay for them, so that Europe's defence guarantees rest on national statutes voted in national chambers. No government may then present American legislation, EU borrowing or common debt as a substitute. What the October waiver showed can happen from Washington cannot be read around from a national statute book.
