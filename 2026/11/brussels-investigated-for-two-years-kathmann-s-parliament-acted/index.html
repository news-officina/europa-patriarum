A Europe of nations.

Opinion

Brussels investigated for two years. Kathmann's parliament acted in two days.

Consumer law is the one power the treaties left fully in national hands, and eight parliaments are right to use it while the Commission's cases sit unresolved. National rules should be a floor Brussels must match, not an obstacle it must erase.

3 November 2026

Two years of investigation, and not one algorithm has been switched off. The Commission opened its cases into addictive design in May 2024. In July 2026 EU tech chief Henna Virkkunen said the DSA provides a clear framework to hold platforms accountable, yet the Commission had produced only preliminary findings, among them one against Meta with a threatened fine of up to 6 percent of global turnover, and no final decision. On 2 November 2026 Barbara Kathmann, a Dutch MP of the PvdA, the Dutch Labour Party, introduced a bill to ban endless scrolling, autoplay and luring notifications as unfair commercial practices, with parliamentarians in Germany, France, Belgium, Luxembourg, Austria, Italy and Portugal moving alongside her. That is not impatience. This is what two years of waiting looks like.

Consumer law is the one instrument the treaties left fully in national hands, and Kathmann is using it. The Unfair Commercial Practices Directive obliges member states to protect consumers from manipulation; it never transferred the power to define what counts as manipulation to a Commission that investigates at its own pace. Her bill would make platforms face a warning first, then a fine, then, on repetition, a switch-off. The Commission's preliminary findings point to the remedy: autoplay and infinite scroll disabled by default, effective screen-time breaks, recommendation systems no longer tuned to maximise engagement. Brussels knows the remedy. It has written it down.

In the same July 2026 statement, Virkkunen added that protecting the physical and mental health of Europeans must be a priority for social media platforms.

We agree, and that is the problem.

Meta still contests the findings.

TikTok promises to fight its findings.

Companies defend themselves while the regulator drafts.

National parliaments legislate.

The strongest case against Kathmann comes from the single market itself. It was clear by September 2026 that countries wanting their own bans depend on the Commission, because only it can impose obligations on companies across all member states. Kathmann and parliamentarians in seven other states are writing them anyway, and eight national laws on addictive design invite forum-shopping, raise compliance costs and, in the end, protect nobody better. There is force in that, and we would take it seriously if the Commission had earned its monopoly. But a regulator with the single market's only enforcement power, after the two years already recounted, is not the guardian of consistency. It is the reason the patchwork exists. The fragmentation argument cuts against the institution that created it.

So the demand runs in two directions. The Commission should close its pending cases under the Digital Services Act with binding design remedies, the ones it has already described, on defined deadlines. In the Digital Fairness Act, expected on 11 November 2026, it should set a common European floor that national consumer law may exceed rather than displace. And national parliaments should legislate now, and not wait. Kathmann herself prefers a European solution, but says it takes too long, so she has turned to the parliament she has. Seven other countries' parliamentarians are moving with her. Brussels has had two years. A parliament needed two days to pick up the remedy the Commission wrote down. The votes that will be asked of us should be the votes that decide it.
